PERSPECTIVE / DATA & DECISIONS

Good analysis starts
with a better question.

A dashboard can show what happened. Useful analysis helps someone understand what to do next.

Joseph Nwankwo · Data analytics & business strategy

My background in supply chain operations shapes how I think about data. A report becomes useful when it helps someone address a concrete problem: a delay, a process that needs attention, or a decision with competing priorities.

That is why I approach analysis as a conversation between a business question and the available evidence. The tools matter, but the purpose comes first.

Start with the decision.

Before opening a spreadsheet or writing a query, define the question. Who will use the findings? What decision are they making? What information would help them choose a next step?

“Improve operations” is a broad goal. “Where are delays occurring in the order process?” is a question that can guide the work. It tells you which records to examine, what to measure, and how to communicate the result.

Check the foundation.

Data quality affects the conclusions you can draw. Missing timestamps, duplicate records, and inconsistent definitions can distort a result before it reaches a dashboard.

Preparation should include checking completeness, confirming what each field represents, and documenting assumptions. When the evidence has limitations, those limitations belong in the analysis.

The goal is to make the evidence easier to understand and the next decision easier to make.

Keep the context visible.

A change in a metric does not explain itself. A longer processing time could reflect a shift in demand, a change in how records are captured, or an operational constraint. Comparing periods or groups is most useful when their differences are understood.

My operations experience reminds me to look beyond the chart and ask how the work happens in practice. The numbers and the people closest to the process can help clarify the same question from different angles.

Communicate a useful next step.

A clear report should explain the question, the evidence, and what the findings suggest. It should also distinguish an observed pattern from an explanation that still needs investigation.

Whether I use Python, SQL, Power BI, or Excel, the aim is the same: turn information into a clear conversation about the next step.
