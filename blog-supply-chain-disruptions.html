Predicting Supply Chain Disruptions with Machine Learning

Oct 8, 2026 Joseph Nwankwo Machine Learning

Can logistics data warn us before a shipment fails? I built and honestly evaluated two ML models to find out.

At a glance

  • Tools: Python, pandas, scikit-learn, SMOTE, Matplotlib
  • Data: shipment lifecycle records — transport metrics, port congestion, inventory, weather, supplier reliability, driver behavior
  • Models: Random Forest vs. Gradient Boosting (stratified 80/20 split)
  • Best result: Gradient Boosting — 73.9% accuracy, 0.849 F1, 98.5% recall

The business problem

Every late shipment, congested port, and misplaced pallet costs money. Operations teams usually react after a disruption. The question I set out to answer: can the logistics data companies already collect predict disruptions before they happen?

The data

I worked with shipment lifecycle data covering transportation metrics and arrival-time variance, traffic and port congestion, inventory levels and warehouse handling times, weather conditions, supplier reliability and lead times, driver behavior and fatigue, shipping costs, and fulfillment status. On top of the raw fields, I engineered congestion scores, driver performance indices, supply chain stress indicators, and operational efficiency metrics.

The approach

  • Missing values handled with median imputation; numerical features scaled with StandardScaler.
  • Class imbalance addressed with SMOTE so the minority (disruption) class is properly learned.
  • Stratified 80/20 train-test split to keep class ratios honest.
  • Trained and compared two models: Random Forest vs. Gradient Boosting, evaluated on accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and ROC-AUC.

The results

ModelAccuracyPrecisionRecallF1ROC-AUC
Random Forest66.71%75.11%82.88%0.7880.503
Gradient Boosting73.87%74.62%98.52%0.8490.492

Gradient Boosting wins on accuracy, recall, and F1 — and 98.5% recall means it catches nearly every disruption, which is what matters when a miss costs more than a false alarm. I'm not hiding the weak spot, though: both ROC-AUC scores sit near 0.50, so the models flag disruptions well but discriminate weakly across thresholds. That's a model that needs more feature work and validation before it goes near production — and knowing where a model isn't ready is part of the job.

Business takeaways

  • Early warning: high-recall flagging lets ops teams intervene on at-risk shipments before failure.
  • Smarter routing: disruption risk scores can feed alternative-routing decisions.
  • Inventory planning: reliability signals inform safety-stock levels per lane and supplier.
  • Supplier scorecards: modeled reliability beats gut feel when renegotiating contracts.

Skills demonstrated

  • End-to-end ML workflow in Python: wrangling, feature engineering, modeling, evaluation.
  • Handling real-world messiness: missing data, class imbalance, honest metric reporting.
  • Statistical judgment: choosing recall-weighted evaluation where misses are expensive.
  • Translating model output into decisions a business can act on.

What's next

Baseline-model comparison, hyperparameter tuning, calibration checks, and richer time-based features. Then the honest question again: does it actually beat a simple rule-based flag?
